# Results Summary

## Research Question

This study examines whether higher military/security spending is associated with later changes in terrorist attack frequency and terrorism-related deaths. The analysis uses a country-year panel dataset covering 2001–2020.

The final model dataset includes 1,382 country-year observations.

## Key Regression Findings

| Model | Outcome | Coefficient | Standard Error | P-value | Significant? |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Model 1A — Year FE attacks model | Log terrorist attacks | 0.1043 | 0.0208 | 5.361e-07 | Yes |
| Model 1B — Year FE deaths model | Log terrorism deaths | 0.1196 | 0.0269 | 8.507e-06 | Yes |
| Model 2A — Country and Year FE attacks model | Log terrorist attacks | -0.0026 | 0.0265 | 0.9232 | No |
| Model 2B — Country and Year FE deaths model | Log terrorism deaths | -0.0301 | 0.0451 | 0.5039 | No |

## Interpretation

The year fixed effects models show a positive and statistically significant relationship between lagged military spending and both terrorist attacks and terrorism deaths. At first glance, this suggests that countries with higher prior-year military spending tend to experience more terrorism.

However, this relationship changes once country fixed effects are added. The country and year fixed effects models compare each country more closely against itself over time rather than mainly comparing high-risk countries to low-risk countries. In these stronger models, lagged military spending is no longer statistically significant for either terrorist attacks or terrorism deaths.

## Main Finding

The results do not provide evidence that higher military spending reduces terrorist attacks or terrorism deaths. Instead, the initial positive association appears to be driven by differences between countries. Countries facing greater terrorism risk also tend to spend more on defense and security, but increases in a country's own military spending are not clearly associated with later reductions in terrorist activity.

## Suggested Paper Wording

The regression results suggest that the relationship between military spending and terrorism outcomes is sensitive to model specification. In models with only year fixed effects, lagged military spending is positively and significantly associated with both terrorist attacks and terrorism deaths. However, after adding country fixed effects, the coefficient on lagged military spending becomes small and statistically insignificant. This indicates that the basic positive association likely reflects cross-country differences in terrorism risk rather than a within-country effect of spending over time. Therefore, the findings do not support the claim that higher military spending independently reduces terrorist activity.

## Limitation

Military expenditure is an imperfect proxy for counterterrorism spending. It includes broad defense spending and does not isolate spending specifically directed toward terrorism prevention, intelligence, law enforcement, border security, homeland security, sanctions enforcement, foreign assistance, or civilian capacity-building. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as evidence about military spending and terrorism outcomes, not as a direct test of all counterterrorism spending.

Recent counterterrorism policy sources reinforce this limitation. The State Department Bureau of Counterterrorism describes counterterrorism as involving diplomacy, partner capacity-building, border and aviation security, sanctions, countering violent extremism, and multilateral cooperation. Brown University's Costs of War project also documents that U.S. counterterrorism activity includes training, assistance, combat operations, and air or drone strikes across many countries. These sources show why aggregate military expenditure should be treated as a broad proxy rather than a precise counterterrorism spending measure.

## Future Research

Future research could improve on this study by using more detailed counterterrorism-specific spending measures. A narrower dataset could separate military spending, homeland security spending, foreign assistance, border security programs, intelligence-related spending, counterterrorism training, sanctions enforcement, and civilian capacity-building. This would allow researchers to test which types of counterterrorism spending are most closely associated with later changes in terrorist attacks and terrorism-related deaths.
